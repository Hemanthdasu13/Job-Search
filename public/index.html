Could you tell if it was wrong?

Most people can describe how they use AI. Far fewer can describe how they would know it had failed them.

I spoke to senior practitioners across sixteen sectors, from investment banking to public healthcare, about AI in real commercial decisions. Most described detailed, enforced rules about what data they were allowed to put in. Not one described a rule about when to believe what comes out.

Exploratory research, eighteen interviews across sixteen sectors. Not a validated instrument.

A pricing recommendation

An analyst asks AI to compare four competitors' pricing and recommend whether to hold or discount. It searches, finds their published rate cards, compares them to the company's own numbers, and recommends holding price. The analysis is clear, well-structured and internally consistent.

Three questions:

What did it actually have? Published rate cards. List prices.

What is it assuming? That list price is what competitors charge.

What might be missing? In enterprise deals nobody charges list. The discounting behaviour that decides this question was never in the data.

The output was not wrong in any way the analyst could see. It was answering a different question from the one that mattered.

Think of a recent piece of work where AI output fed into something that mattered. A decision, a recommendation, an analysis someone else relied on. What was it?

You said: “”

The output was checkable. The inputs were not.

This is the most common position in the study. People check what the system produced, because that is what is in front of them. Almost nobody checks what it had to work from, because that is invisible.

A group finance director described building a pension model with AI over a weekend. It looked sensible. He then built a separate reconciliation to test it against, and found around six errors:

I could have just looked at the first output and I thought, yeah, that says I'm going to have a comfortable retirement. I've got enough. My plan is on track.

He did not find them because something looked wrong. He found them because he built something to check against.

Worth trying: before accepting an analysis, ask what the system actually had, what it is assuming, and what might be missing. That sequence came from one of the participants, not from the research design.

One more thing from the study: in twelve of the interviews, four people had a rule they could state for when to trust AI output. Eight had nothing equivalent. Of the four, three had taken a decision rule they already used for something else and applied it to AI without anyone telling them to.

You described a constructed check, not a careful read.

That is what the practitioners in this study who caught real errors had in common. Not more attention, but something independent for the output to fail against: a reconciliation, a second model, a colleague who knows the area, a check written before the answer was produced.

The study also found this is the first thing to go under time pressure, which is when high-stakes decisions usually get made.
