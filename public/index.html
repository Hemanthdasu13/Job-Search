Convincing and correct look the same.

AI can give you a wrong answer and leave you sure it is right.

Not wrong in a way you would catch.Wrong in a way that arrives looking exactly like the answers that were fine.

I spoke to senior practitioners across sixteen sectors, from investment banking to public healthcare, about AI in real commercial decisions. Most described detailed, enforced rules about what data they were allowed to put in.

Not one described a rule for when to believe what came out.

The people who handled it best were not the ones who knew the most about AI.

They were the ones who had something to put the answer against when they could not tell.

We agree with people who sound certain.We agree faster when they are senior.

AI learnt that from us.It has just become very good at it.

Maybe the most human thing about an AI answer is not the answer.It is us believing it.

A pricing recommendation

An analyst asks AI to compare four competitors' pricing and recommend whether to hold or discount. It searches, finds their published rate cards, compares them to the company's own numbers, and recommends holding price. The analysis is clear, well-structured and internally consistent.

Three questions:

What did it actually have? Published rate cards. List prices.

What is it assuming? That list price is what competitors charge.

What might be missing? In enterprise deals nobody charges list. The discounting behaviour that decides this question was never in the data.

The output was not wrong in any way the analyst could see. It was answering a different question from the one that mattered.

From what you described —

Not things you got wrong. Things other people kept running into, that did not come up in what you described.

    The output was checkable. The inputs were not.

    This is the most common position, by some distance. People check what the system produced, because that is what is in front of them. Almost nobody checks what it had to work from, because that is invisible.

    A group finance director described building a pension model with AI over a weekend. It looked sensible, and it would have been easy to read the first output, see a comfortable retirement and stop there. A separate reconciliation was built to test it against, and it turned up around six errors.

    They were not found because something looked wrong. They were found because something was built to check against.

    Worth trying: before accepting an analysis, ask what the system actually had, what it is assuming, and what might be missing. That sequence came from one of the participants, not from the research design.

    One more thing: of twelve, four had a rule they could state for when to trust AI output. Eight had nothing equivalent. Of the four, three had taken a rule they already used for something else and pointed it at AI, with nobody telling them to.
